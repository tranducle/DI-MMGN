      Abstract. Defacement attacks currently pose one of the most signifi-
      cant threats to web applications. Attackers often utilize this method to
      leave messages and signatures, which serve as a form of propaganda or
      a demonstration of power. However, existing detection methods mainly
      focus on content-based analysis, which can consume substantial compu-
      tational resources, require extended processing times, and reduce accu-
      racy. Furthermore, these methods typically overlook the attackers’ sig-
      natures—an indicator that may seem simple but is highly effective for
      detection. This paper proposes a defacement detection framework that
      integrates signature analysis with an attack detection model, combining
      image, text, and HTML features. This framework is capable of effectively
      handling both static and dynamic web pages. Experimental evaluations
      conducted on a dataset of 20,255 collected signatures and 96,220 web
      pages demonstrate that our approach achieves an accuracy rate of up
      to 99.23%, with a False Alarm Rate (FAR) of only 0.38%, surpassing
      existing detection methods.


1   Introduction
Web defacement is a type of attack targeting web servers, typically involving
alterations to the content, layout, color scheme, or images of a website [1,2]. The
primary purpose of such attacks is to highlight security vulnerabilities to the
website owner. However, certain groups also exploit defacement attacks to sab-
otage websites, damage their reputation, or deceive users by spreading false or
misleading information. Hackers commonly deface websites to leave behind their
signatures and messages, resembling street art in the digital realm. Many at-
tacker groups use these defaced web pages as a canvas to showcase their presence
and achievements, often as a form of self-promotion or ideological expression.
    Web defacement detection techniques are commonly divided into anomaly-
based, signature-based, and Machine Learning (ML) approaches. Traditional
anomaly-based methods, such as checksum and DIFF comparison, are simple
and fast but only suitable for static websites. In contrast, advanced techniques
like statistical analysis, genetic programming, and ML handle dynamic websites
more effectively but require higher computational resources. Signature-based
detection relies on known attack patterns for quick identification but cannot de-
tect novel attacks. Despite extensive research, few solutions combine the speed
of signature-based methods with the adaptability of anomaly-based techniques.
    This section reviews prior studies on both approaches. Signature-based meth-
ods rely on predefined patterns derived from previously known attacks, such as
specific keywords, URLs, or HTML structures. These techniques enable fast
and accurate detection of previously observed attacks but face challenges in
identifying new or modified attack variants. Harahap et al. [3] developed a low-
computational-cost method for detecting website defacement in university do-
mains using predefined attack signatures. Their approach utilizes patterns such
as keywords, URLs, or HTML structures. However, its dependence on known
signatures and limited capability in analyzing dynamic content or multimedia
elements reduces its effectiveness against novel attacks. To overcome these limi-
tations, hybrid techniques have been explored. Hoang et al. [4] proposed a system
that combines supervised ML with signature-based integrity verification using
hash functions. Although effective in detecting tampering in static HTML con-
tent, the hash-based approach is sensitive to minor changes, which may lead to
a high false positive rate when applied to dynamic or multimedia content.
    Anomaly-based detection models, particularly those utilizing ML, have at-
tracted considerable research interest. Burruss et al. [5] used a finite mixture
model to classify attackers into low- and high-volume groups, offering behav-
ioral insights but lacking real-time detection capabilities. Almomani et al. [6]
proposed an Enhanced URL Defacement Detection model using Particle Swarm
Optimization (PSO) to improve feature selection, achieving 92.5% accuracy on
static datasets. However, its reliance on URL features limits performance on dy-
namic websites and multimedia content. Wu et al. [7] applied a Support Vector
Machine (SVM) to HTML features, reaching 87.3% accuracy but meeting with
high FAR due to fixed thresholds and weak performance on visually rich content.
Hoang et al. [8] employed BiLSTM on webpage text yet ignored HTML struc-
ture and embedded media, reducing effectiveness on complex pages. Nguyen
et al. [9] combined text and screenshot data but only averaged outputs from
separate models without integrating cross-modal interactions, limiting overall
performance.
    The limitations of current website defacement detection solutions include
(1) focusing mainly on URL content while neglecting actual webpage content,
leading to low effectiveness; (2) being suitable only for static websites; (3) high
false alarm rates due to fixed detection thresholds; (4) inadequate processing of
images and HTML structure; and (5) limited feature integration, relying only
on separate evaluations of individual data types. To address these issues, this
paper makes three key contributions as follows:
 – the construction of a comprehensive dataset containing over 96,000 web
   pages with image, text, and HTML data, along with more than 20,200 unique
   defacement signatures for improved signature-based detection;
 – the proposal of a multimodal detection model that combines image, text,
   and HTML features, outperforming prior anomaly-based methods;
 – the development of a two-stage detection framework that integrates signature-
   based and multimodal analysis to enhance accuracy, reduce false positives,
   and improve processing speed.
   The rest of this paper is organized as follows: Section 2 introduces the pro-
posed approach, Section 3 presents and analyzes the experimental results. In
Section 4, we have a conclusion and suggest the future direction of this study.


2     Proposed Methodologies
This section introduces the proposed robust two-stage framework for the web
defacement detection model, RTFDD. The model combines signature-based de-
tection in the first stage with a deep learning-based detection approach in the
second stage. If no known signature is detected during the initial phase, the sys-
tem proceeds to the deep learning model, which leverages multimodal features
- including visual, textual, and HTML characteristics - to identify potential de-
facement attacks.

2.1   Overview
Figure 1 illustrates the overall architecture of the proposed RTFDD (Robust
Two-Stage Framework for Web Defacement Detection). The hybrid framework
consists of two main stages: a signature-based detection module and an anomaly-
based deep learning module. In the first stage, incoming webpages are first pro-
cessed and compared against a database of predefined defacement signatures. If
a match is found, the system immediately raises a defacement alert. If no match
is detected, the input proceeds to the second stage, where a multimodal deep
learning model is used for anomaly detection. In the anomaly-based stage, the
webpage content is divided into three modalities: HTML structure, textual con-
tent, and screenshot image. Each modality is processed separately through ded-
icated modules: HTML features are extracted using a pretrained BERT model
applied to the DOM tree, text features are processed through a text encoder, and
image features are extracted via a CNN-based model. These features are then
fused through a transformer-based integration layer, followed by a multi-layer
perceptron classifier to make the final defacement decision.

2.2   Signature-based Attack Detection
The first stage of the RTFDD framework leverages a signature-based detection
mechanism to rapidly identify known web defacement attacks. This stage is de-
signed to be lightweight and computationally efficient, making it suitable for

real-time monitoring of large-scale web environments. To implement this mech-
anism, a comprehensive signature database has been constructed, consisting of
20,255 unique signatures extracted from previously recorded web defacement
incidents. These signatures were collected from publicly available defacement
archives, security forums, and manually labeled datasets. Each signature en-
codes distinct indicators of compromise, including specific keywords commonly
used by attackers (e.g., "Hacked by", "Owned by"). During detection, the incom-
ing webpage is scanned and tokenized to extract relevant textual and structural
features. These features are then matched against the signature database using
a fast matching algorithm based on approximate string matching and rule-based
pattern recognition. If a match is found, the system immediately classifies the
page as defaced, and an alert is generated. This early detection step allows the
system to block well-known attacks quickly, reducing the burden on the more
resource-intensive deep learning model in the second stage.


2.3   Anomaly-based Attack Detection Using Feature Fusion

In the second stage of the RTFDD framework, an anomaly-based detection ap-
proach is employed to identify web defacement attacks that cannot be captured
by known signatures. This stage utilizes a multimodal deep learning architecture
that integrates features extracted from the HTML structure, textual content, and
webpage screenshots.




          Fig. 2. DOM Tree Processing with Pre-order and Max 5 tokens.


    HTML Feature Extraction: The HTML content of each webpage is first
parsed into a DOM tree, where each node contains both tag information and
associated textual content. To reduce noise, non-informative elements such as
<script> and <style> tags are removed, while useful attributes like class and
id are retained. Given the complexity and size of DOM trees, the structure is
then divided into overlapping subtrees using a sliding window based on a fixed
number of tokens. This preserves local context and prevents the loss of important
structural relationships.
    Each subtree is serialized and encoded using a transformer-based encoder
with modified positional embeddings to reflect parent-child and sibling relation-
ships between nodes. In particular, despite the initial cleaning process, many
DOM trees still consist of thousands of tokens and data points, many of which
lack semantic significance. This poses a challenge when feeding the data into
modern deep learning models. To address this, the DOM tree is further divided
into overlapping subtrees using a sliding window approach based on a fixed to-
ken size m. Unlike conventional text-based sliding windows, which often disrupt
crucial parent-child relationships, this method keeps semantically related nodes
intact. The segmentation process begins at the root of the DOM tree and fol-
lows a pre-order traversal. The first subtree is constructed by collecting up to m
tokens. Then, additional subtrees are created by gradually expanding the cur-
rent subtree with newly encountered nodes, while older, less relevant nodes are
pruned. This dynamic expansion and pruning mechanism help retain meaningful
structural context throughout the segmentation. For instance, as illustrated in
Figure 2, the tree is traversed in pre-order with a maximum token limit of 5. At
each iteration, the subtree is updated by incorporating new nodes and discarding
those that fall outside the sliding window. The final subtree at any point con-
tains the most recently added nodes along with relevant nodes preserved from
earlier steps.
    The model is further pretrained based on Bert-base-uncase [10] on unlabeled
HTML webpages using the Masked Language Modeling (MLM) objective to
learn structural and semantic representations specific to web layout. The core
of this encoder is the self-attention mechanism [11], where the model learns to
create representations of a token by aggregating information from other tokens
in the input sequence. Specifically, this paper first randomly selects 15% of token
positions, then repeatedly samples DOM nodes and adds all their tokens to the
mask until the total number of masked tokens reaches the desired 15%. As in
BERT, for a chosen position, (1) replace it with a special token [MASK] 80% of
the time, (2) replace it with a random token 10% of the time, and (3) leave it
unchanged in the remaining cases as described in Figure 3.




                         Fig. 3. Pretraining with HTML.


   Text and Image Feature Extraction: Textual data extracted from the
webpage body is preprocessed and tokenized by Tokenizer [12]. A Bidirectional
Long Short-Term Memory (BiLSTM) network is used to encode the sequence of
tokens, capturing both forward and backward contextual dependencies. The out-
put is a dense vector representation reflecting the semantic structure of the text
content. Webpage screenshots are standardized to a fixed resolution of 224×224
pixels, and their pixel values are normalized to the range [0, 1]. The preprocessed
images are then passed through an EfficientNetB0 [13] to extract visual features
that capture layout, color distribution, and embedded visual elements relevant
to potential defacement indicators.
    Fusion Feature: The feature vectors obtained from the HTML (denoted as
H), text (T), and image (I) branches are concatenated into a unified set of feature
vectors: F = H ∪T ∪I. These vectors are passed into a transformer encoder with
self-attention, which allows the model to compute interactions across different
modalities, learning joint representations that integrate structural, textual, and
visual cues. The output of the transformer is then fed into a multi-layer per-
ceptron (MLP) classifier, which outputs a binary label indicating whether the
webpage has been defaced or is benign. This multimodal design addresses key
limitations of previous methods by enabling the model to detect sophisticated
defacement attempts that manipulate layout, alter textual content, or modify
visual presentation. It ensures robustness across both static and dynamic web-
pages and enhances generalization to previously unseen attack patterns.


3     Experiments and Evaluation

3.1   Dataset Processing

Previous studies in this domain have highlighted several limitations, such as the
use of statistical methods without access to appropriate datasets, reliance on lim-
ited or confidential data, and a narrow focus on specific modalities such as text
or image, which reduces detection effectiveness. Moreover, prior research has not
introduced any comprehensive signature datasets specifically designed for deface-
ment detection. To ensure a fair and reliable evaluation, we re-implemented rep-
resentative detection methods and developed a customized dataset for training
and testing purposes. This dataset includes both normal and defaced websites:
normal samples were collected from top-ranked Alexa websites [14], while defaced
samples were obtained from Zone-h.org [15]. Using a toolset based on Selenium
WebDriver [16] and BeautifulSoup, we extracted HTML content, textual infor-
mation, and page screenshots from each web page. The final dataset comprises
57,120 labeled normal samples and 39,100 defaced samples. From the 39,100 de-
faced samples, we further extracted a collection of 20,255 distinct defacement
signatures, which serve as the foundation for the signature-based detection com-
ponent of our system.


3.2   Experimental Setups

For the pretraining phase, the model utilizes BERT-base-uncased as the back-
bone and performs fine-tuning with a hidden layer size of 768. The model is
pretrained using unlabeled HTML data extracted from the prepared dataset for
5 epochs, with a batch size of 16 and a learning rate of 2e−5 . For the classifica-
tion phase, a deep learning-based model is employed. The training parameters
of the classification model are summarized in Table 1. Notably, the final layers
of the network consist of fully connected layers, with 256 units in the first layer,
followed by 2 output units representing the two classes: defaced and benign.

3.3   Results and Analysis

For the signature-based classification phase, the model utilizes the collected sig-
nature dataset. It performs pattern matching between the signatures and the in-
put web content to determine whether the page has been defaced. Pages flagged
as suspicious are then passed to the deep learning-based detection model for
further verification.
    Figure 4 illustrates comparable accuracy trends, highlighting the robustness
of our deep learning-based model. The plotted curves demonstrate a consistent
pattern, featuring rapid convergence in the initial stages and steady improve-
ment thereafter. The close correlation between training and validation results
reinforces the model’s generalization ability and its effectiveness in accurate de-
tection.
    For the deep learning-based classification phase, the paper utilizes the con-
structed dataset as described in Section 3.1, with a train-test split ratio of 80:20.
The study evaluates several previously proposed methods on this newly con-
structed dataset, including Random Forest [4], BiLSTM [8], and BiLSTM +
EfficientNet [9]; as well as the method by Wu et al. [7]. In addition, the pa-
per evaluates two proposed deep learning-based detection models: one based on
BERT and another based on feature fusion. Furthermore, the paper assesses the
proposed two-stage RTFDD framework, which combines signature-based detec-
tion with the deep learning-based feature fusion model. The results are evaluated
based on two main criteria: detection accuracy, including related metrics such
as Recall, Precision, and F1-score, and execution performance, which includes
inference time and detection speed. Since the proposed model incorporates a
signature-based detection phase, the detection time is specifically measured on
the 20% test subset to reflect realistic performance under deployment conditions.

patterns. This hybrid mechanism allows the system to leverage both precise pat-

Moreover, it delivers acceptable processing times relative to traditional machine
learning approaches while offering substantially superior accuracy.


4   Conclusion

In this study, the authors proposed a new method for detecting web defacement
attacks based on a two-stage model named RTFDD, which combines signature-
based analysis with deep learning techniques that integrate visual, textual, and
HTML features. An effective framework was introduced for extracting features
from HTML, a data source that has been underutilized in previous research.
During the process, the authors also constructed a comprehensive dataset com-
prising over 20,200 defacement signatures and more than 96,000 webpages, each
with associated screenshots, textual, and HTML data. Experimental evaluation
conducted on a dataset consisting of 20,225 signatures and 96,220 webpages
confirmed the superior performance of the proposed method, achieving an ac-
curacy of 99.23% and a false positive rate of only 0.38%. This detection ap-
proach enhances accuracy while maintaining fast computational performance,
due to the incorporation of signature matching in the detection process. How-
ever, the deep learning component remains resource-intensive, which may limit
the scalability of the model in certain real-world web security deployments. Fu-
ture research directions include optimizing the deep learning module to minimize
computational overhead, thereby facilitating deployment in resource-constrained
or real-time web monitoring environments. Furthermore, extending the dataset
to encompass multilingual content and a wider variety of website architectures is
expected to enhance the generalizability and resilience of the proposed approach
against emerging and sophisticated web defacement techniques.
